PHINDER investigates how III–V nanowire devices and indium-phosphide (InP) photonic integrated circuits can form a sensing and processing platform. The technology combines local optoelectronic computation with optical links and programmable photonic networks.

{{< research-block figure="optical-link" label="01 / Devices and connections" >}}
## Nanoscale optoelectronic building blocks

Semiconductor nanowires can act as photodetectors, light emitters, transistors and wavelength-selective optical elements. Their small footprint brings detection, signal integration and activation close together. [Deterministic placement and fabrication techniques](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mee.2025.112363) allow different nanowires to be positioned and contacted in designed circuits.

## Light connects the nodes

[Flodgren and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsphotonics.4c01375) demonstrated direct on-chip optical communication between individual nanowire devices. Light from an emitter reaches a neighbouring receiver without an electrical signal wire connecting the two.

This makes geometry and optical properties part of the computing architecture. [Optical-broadcasting research](https://doi.org/10.1515/nanoph-2025-0035) explores how nanowire position, orientation and wavelength affect coupling and network connectivity.

## Photonic integration for larger networks

PHINDER will investigate integration of III–V nanowire nodes with InP photonic integrated circuits. Waveguides, wavelength-division multiplexing and optical fan-out provide routes to distribute signals among many nodes. Semiconductor optical amplifiers and nonlinear stages can support weighted connections and processing on chip.

The [InP multilayer-network architecture of Shi, Calabretta and Stabile](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0066350) provides relevant background for this approach. It describes a photonic network architecture and performance analysis, rather than an already completed PHINDER circuit.

## Co-designed from device to application {#co-design}

The physical device, photonic network and learning algorithm cannot be optimised independently. PHINDER combines component models, system-level digital twins and application simulations. Measurements from fabricated prototypes will feed back into the models and algorithms.

A surrogate model learns to emulate an expensive simulation and reconstruction chain. It can then accelerate exploration of design choices and provide gradients for optimisation. The [AIDO workflow](https://doi.org/10.3390/particles8020047) illustrates this iterative approach for detector design; [robustness-oriented photonic training](https://doi.org/10.1364/OE.588175) adds complementary background on hardware-aware optimisation.

## Research objectives

The project targets picosecond-scale optical event detection, sub-nanosecond latency and improved energy efficiency through closely coupled sensing and processing. These are research objectives, not specifications of an available device.
